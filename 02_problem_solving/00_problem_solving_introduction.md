# Problem Solving – Quick Revision Notes

## 1. What Is Problem Solving?

**Problem solving** is the process of finding answers to difficult or complicated personal and professional issues.

- It is a core skill for making decisions at work and in everyday life.
- It can involve a quick decision in a meeting or a complex project lasting months.
- Good problem solvers use a deliberate, structured approach rather than jumping straight to solutions.
- Problem-solving is both an **art and a science**: structure guides the process, while judgment and application matter in practice.

> Great problem solvers are made, not born.

## 2. The Hypothesis-Led Approach

A **hypothesis** is your first best guess about a possible solution, based on limited evidence or information. It gives the discussion and investigation a starting point.

A structured approach helps you:

1. **Define** the problem using appropriate tools and templates.
2. **Structure** and break down the problem.
3. **Plan** the work needed to reach a recommended solution.

A hypothesis is a starting point to test and refine—not a conclusion to defend.

## 3. What Makes a Good Problem Solver?

### Three core practices

- **Structure the problem:** Break it into clear parts and decide what to address.
- **Apply the 80/20 principle:** Focus on the 20% of issues or analyses likely to drive 80% of the value.
- **Plan implementation:** Consider stakeholders, rollout, practical constraints and possible pitfalls—not just the answer.

### Reflect on your current approach

| Area | Questions to ask |
|---|---|
| Actions | What steps do I take? |
| Stakeholders | Whom do I involve? |
| Approach | How do I adapt to different problems? |
| Toolkit | Which resources do I use? |
| Strengths | What do I do well? |
| Opportunities | What could I improve? |

## 4. Collaboration Improves the Answer

Great problem solving uses the **collective power of the group**.

- Avoid placing the entire problem-solving burden on one person.
- Involve people with different perspectives and expertise.
- Put the problem, constraints, stakeholders and proposed structure in writing so everyone can align.
- If everyone agrees too quickly, challenge the group's thinking.

**Useful challenge techniques**
- **Red team / blue team:** Examine an issue from opposing perspectives.
- **Devil's advocate:** Assign someone to challenge the preferred idea and test whether it is really the best option.

Collaboration can improve both the quality and speed of the answer, while also building commitment to the solution.

## 5. Match the Approach to the Time Available

| Time horizon | Main focus |
|---|---|
| **In the moment** | Define the problem, identify constraints, align stakeholders and scope the solution set. |
| **1–2 weeks** | Prioritize the issues and analyses that matter most; time is still limited. |
| **Long-term project** | Build a work plan, map dependencies, align stakeholders and identify early decisions that may affect later stages. |

**Key idea:** The longer the time horizon, the more important it becomes to plan dependencies and decisions. More time often also means greater complexity and value at stake.

## 6. Understand the Type of Problem

### Analytical vs. conceptual

| Type | Focus | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Analytical** | Quantitative; requires analysis or calculation | Deciding how much to spend on groceries each month |
| **Conceptual** | Qualitative; requires judgment, framing or communication | Deciding how to raise an important issue with a supervisor |

A long-term project may contain both analytical and conceptual problems.

### Convergent vs. divergent thinking

| Thinking style | Purpose | Typical activities |
|---|---|---|
| **Convergent** | Narrow options and move towards a best answer | Eliminate options, identify key issues and establish an initial hypothesis |
| **Divergent** | Expand the solution space and discover possibilities | Generate a wide range of opportunities and unique ideas |

**Balance matters:**
- Using analysis when creative thinking is needed can limit solutions.
- Staying in idea-generation mode for too long can prevent a decision.
- Effective problem solving knows when to explore and when to narrow down.

## 7. Five Problem-Solving Lenses (Flexons)

**Flexons** are flexible problem-solving languages or lenses. They help reframe ambiguous, complex problems and reveal solutions that may be hidden by familiar ways of thinking.

Using multiple lenses can produce richer insights. Each lens has assumptions and blind spots, so no single lens is sufficient for every problem.

### 1. Networks

**Focus:** Relationships and connections between entities.

- Map relationships, influence, trust and interactions.
- Identify important connections, clusters and influential people.
- Use the map to understand how relationships may evolve and where influence can be applied.

**Example:** Map connections between physicians to identify influential groups when introducing a new medicine.

### 2. Evolutionary

**Focus:** Generate variety, test alternatives, select and retain promising options.

- Useful when many variables are uncertain or difficult to control.
- Create multiple ideas, prototypes or business models.
- Run small, low-cost experiments.
- Learn from results and filter out weaker options.

**Example:** Test several toothpaste variants with selected customer groups and refine the product based on feedback.

**Core cycle:** Variation → Selection → Retention.

### 3. Decision-agent

**Focus:** How individuals, teams or organizations make choices based on beliefs, motives, incentives and strategies.

- Identify the relevant decision-makers or agents.
- Understand what each agent wants and believes.
- Consider how one agent's actions affect the payoffs and strategies of others.
- Account for both rational decisions and biases or cognitive shortcuts.

**Example:** When evaluating factory closures, consider the likely responses and incentives of customers, unions and governments—not only plant capacity and input costs.

### 4. System-dynamics

**Focus:** How variables interact and how decisions create effects over time.

- Map cause-and-effect relationships.
- Understand flows and accumulations of money, materials, energy or information.
- Look for bottlenecks, delays and cascading effects.
- Use the map to identify where an intervention can reduce the problem.

**Example:** Model an oil spill as flows through pipes and containment vessels to identify ways to reduce the amount reaching the ocean.

### 5. Information-processing

**Focus:** How information is collected, shared, processed and used to make decisions.

- Examine what information decision-makers receive.
- Consider the cost and efficiency of processing information.
- Understand how organizational processes affect deliberation and decisions.
- Identify whether decision rules suppress useful information or debate.

**Example:** A private-equity investment committee discovers that its near-unanimity rule discourages members from speaking up, allowing risky investments to pass. Changing the voting rule could encourage richer debate.

### Flexons at a glance

| Flexon | Main question |
|---|---|
| Networks | Who is connected to whom, and where is influence? |
| Evolutionary | What alternatives can we generate, test and improve? |
| Decision-agent | What do the decision-makers want, believe and choose? |
| System-dynamics | How do variables affect one another over time? |
| Information-processing | What information is used, and how do decision processes shape the outcome? |

## 8. Applying Multiple Flexons

The lenses become especially useful when combined.

### Example: Improving research productivity

- **Networks:** Map collaboration between researchers and identify gaps in knowledge-sharing.
- **Evolutionary:** Increase the variety of ideas and improve how ideas are tested and selected.
- **Decision-agent:** Examine whether individual incentives align with organizational goals; understand why teams continue or abandon research paths.

The analysis can also distinguish:
- **Type I error:** Pursuing a development path unlikely to produce a profitable solution.
- **Type II error:** Failing to pursue a path that could produce a profitable solution.

### Example: Predicting the future of a telecommunications business

- **Decision-agent:** Explore the incentives and likely strategies of customers, competitors and industry players.
- **Networks:** Map relationships among customers, service providers, equipment makers, chip suppliers, operating systems and applications.
- **System-dynamics:** Identify data-flow bottlenecks and understand how demand, usage and capacity affect investment decisions.

Different lenses reveal different parts of a complex situation. Their findings must then be synthesized into a coherent view.

## 9. Key Takeaways

1. **Pause before solving:** Define and structure the problem instead of jumping to an answer.
2. **Choose the right thinking style:** Identify whether the problem is analytical or conceptual, and whether it needs divergent or convergent thinking.
3. **Use time deliberately:** Match the depth of analysis and planning to the available time.
4. **Prioritize:** Apply the 80/20 principle to focus on the issues that create the most value.
5. **Collaborate:** Involve stakeholders and challenge group assumptions.
6. **Think about implementation:** A good recommendation must be realistic and actionable.
7. **Reframe complex problems:** Use multiple flexons to uncover perspectives and solutions that one familiar approach may miss.
