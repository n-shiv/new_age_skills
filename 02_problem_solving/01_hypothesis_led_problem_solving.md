# Hypothesis-Led Problem Solving – Quick Revision Notes

## 1. Different Structured Problem-Solving Approaches

The approach should depend on the **problem, context and available information**. Five commonly used approaches are:

| Approach | General focus |
|---|---|
| **Hypothesis-led** | Start with an initial hypothesis and test it systematically |
| **Domain IP-led** | Draw on existing domain knowledge and intellectual property |
| **Advanced analytics** | Use data and analytical methods to understand the problem |
| **Design thinking** | Understand user needs and develop, test and refine ideas |
| **Engineering** | Apply engineering principles and methods to solve the problem |

- One approach may fit better than another depending on the situation.
- Different approaches can also be combined.
- A structured approach can be used for large projects as well as everyday decisions.

### Shared characteristics

Regardless of the approach, effective problem solving is:

1. **Focused:** Starts with a clear problem statement.
2. **Impact-oriented:** Concentrates on the outcome that matters.
3. **Stakeholder-aware:** Considers the people affected by or involved in the decision.
4. **Fact-based:** Uses evidence rather than unsupported assumptions.
5. **Synthesis-driven:** Connects findings into a clear overall answer.

## 2. Hypothesis-Led Problem Solving

**Definition:** A structured approach that begins by defining the problem and forming initial hypotheses about what may be true. The work is then organized around testing those hypotheses to reach a better answer efficiently.

### Why use it?

- Breaks large, ambiguous problems into manageable parts.
- Helps prioritize the work and evidence needed.
- Can be applied across many types of problems.
- Scales to different levels of complexity.
- Does not require deep specialist expertise to get started.
- Creates room for creative thinking within a clear structure.

**Important:** A hypothesis is an assertion to prove or disprove—not a fact or a final answer.

## 3. The Seven Steps


| Step | Focus | Guiding question |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Define the problem** | Impact | What do we need to know or decide? |
| **2. Structure the problem** | Break it down | What are the key elements or possible explanations? |
| **3. Prioritize issues** | Speed | Which issues matter most? |
| **4. Develop an analysis / work plan** | Efficiency | Where and how should we spend our time? |
| **5. Conduct analyses** | Evidence | What are we trying to prove or disprove? |
| **6. Synthesize findings** | “So what?” | What do the findings mean together? |
| **7. Develop recommendations** | Action | What should we do? |

### Step 1 – Define the problem

Create a concise, specific problem statement before collecting data or building models.

Clarify:
- What exactly are we trying to solve?
- What decision must be made?
- What are the constraints and dependencies?
- How quickly is an answer needed?
- How precise does the answer need to be?
- What areas are in scope or off limits?
- Who is the decision-maker and who are the stakeholders?

**Why it matters:** Different stakeholders may have different interpretations of the problem. A clear statement aligns everyone and prevents work in the wrong direction.

### Step 2 – Structure the problem

Break the overall problem into logical, manageable components using **logic trees**.

- Disaggregate the problem into smaller parts.
- Explore more than one way to structure it; different structures can reveal different insights.
- Use the branches to identify what needs investigation.
- Assign components to team members where appropriate.

**Example – Profit tree**

- Profit depends on revenue and cost.
- Revenue can be broken into price and quantity.
- Costs can be broken into fixed and variable costs.
- A return-on-assets view also considers the asset or investment base.

A logic tree helps reveal which component may explain the overall result.

### Step 3 – Prioritize issues

Not every branch deserves equal time and effort.

Prioritize issues based on:
- **Impact:** How much does this factor affect the desired outcome?
- **Influence:** Can we realistically change or move this factor?

Focus on issues that are both important and actionable. Avoid spending too much time on factors that have little impact or cannot be influenced.

### Step 4 – Develop an analysis / work plan

Turn the priorities into a practical plan.

Include:
- Analyses or activities required.
- Ownership and responsibilities.
- Timelines and expected deliverables.
- Stakeholders who need to be involved.
- The level of detail and precision required.

The work plan should reflect the **stakes, time available and decision requirements**.

**Iterative planning:** Do not treat the plan as fixed. Update it as new evidence and learning emerge. A quick one-day or one-hour answer can be useful as an initial direction, even if a high-stakes decision later needs deeper validation.

### Step 5 – Conduct analyses

Use evidence to test the hypotheses and understand the prioritized issues.

Good practices:
- Start with simple heuristics and explanatory statistics.
- Understand the shape and scope of the problem before using complex models.
- Check whether the data is correct, sound and meaningful.
- Use test-and-learn methods, A/B testing or exploratory data analysis when appropriate.
- Select analytical tools based on the problem—not because a tool is fashionable.

**Machine learning and advanced analytics:** These can reveal patterns that may otherwise be difficult to see, but they do not replace good problem definition, sound model design or awareness of algorithmic bias.

### Step 6 – Synthesize findings

Analysis is not the same as an answer.

- Bring individual findings together.
- Identify the overall message and implications.
- Explain what the evidence means for the decision.
- Build a clear story that answers: **“So what?”**

### Step 7 – Develop recommendations

Convert the synthesis into a clear recommendation.

- State what should be done.
- Explain why the recommendation follows from the evidence.
- Make the next action clear.
- Communicate the recommendation in a way that helps the decision-maker act.

**Key principle:** Analysis alone does not solve a problem. A recommendation must lead to informed action.

## 4. Iteration, Bias and Uncertainty

### Iterative problem solving

The seven steps provide structure, but they are not a rigid, one-way sequence.

- New evidence may change the hypothesis.
- Analysis may reveal that the problem needs to be restructured.
- Priorities and work plans may need to change.
- Revisit earlier steps as learning develops.

### Common cognitive biases

| Bias | What it means / why to watch for it |
|---|---|
| **Anchoring** | Giving too much weight to the first information or assumption |
| **Overoptimism** | Being excessively positive about outcomes |
| **Availability bias** | Assuming a current problem resembles a familiar past problem because that example comes readily to mind |
| **Hierarchy / sunflower bias** | People adjust their views based on the seniority or authority of others |

**Ways to reduce bias**
- Invite different perspectives and encourage debate.
- Let junior team members speak before senior members.
- Ask: “In what context was this previously true?”
- Check whether assumptions from the past still apply to the current situation.
- Design team discussions so people can challenge ideas openly.

### Handling uncertainty

Acknowledge uncertainty throughout the process:

- **Definition:** Identify the sources of uncertainty and whether they matter.
- **Structuring:** Recognize that different branches may have different levels of certainty.
- **Prioritization:** Consider both likelihood and potential impact.
- **Synthesis and recommendations:** State how certain the findings are and the conditions under which a recommendation is likely to hold.

Avoid presenting an uncertain conclusion as an absolute fact. Be clear about what is known, what is uncertain and what assumptions the recommendation depends on.

## 5. Design Thinking and Its Relationship to Structured Problem Solving

**Design thinking** starts with empathy for the user.

Typical characteristics:
- Observe users in real situations.
- Understand their experiences, pain points and needs.
- Use those observations to define the problem.
- Generate multiple ideas and possible solutions.
- Build prototypes and gather feedback.
- Iterate based on what is learned.

### Design thinking vs. classic structured problem solving

| Aspect | Hypothesis-led / classic approach | Design thinking |
|---|---|---|
| Problem definition | Starts by clarifying the situation and decision | Uses deep user empathy and observation to understand the problem |
| Early thinking | Forms an initial hypothesis and structures the issue | Keeps the solution space open through divergent thinking |
| Typical output | Often a model or evidence-backed recommendation | Often a prototype or tangible concept |
| Learning | Tests hypotheses through analysis and evidence | Tests ideas with users and iterates based on feedback |

**They are complementary, not competing approaches.**

- Both benefit from clear problem definition, creativity and iteration.
- Design thinking reinforces empathy and user-centered understanding.
- Structured problem solving reinforces logic, prioritization and evidence.
- Teams need clarity on roles, process and expected outcomes, along with respect for each approach.

## 6. Quick Revision Checklist

Before moving into analysis, ask:

- [ ] Is the problem statement specific and agreed upon?
- [ ] Are constraints, dependencies and stakeholders clear?
- [ ] Have I broken the problem into logical components?
- [ ] Have I considered alternative ways to structure it?
- [ ] Am I prioritizing high-impact issues that can be influenced?
- [ ] Is the work plan realistic for the time and precision required?
- [ ] Are the analyses testing clear hypotheses?
- [ ] Have I checked data quality and possible biases?
- [ ] Have I synthesized the findings into a clear message?
- [ ] Does the recommendation lead to a practical action?

## 7. Key Takeaways

1. **Choose the approach to fit the context.** Different approaches can be combined.
2. **Start with a clear problem statement.** Misunderstanding the problem wastes time and effort.
3. **Break problems into logical parts.** Logic trees help reveal the components and possible causes.
4. **Prioritize impact and influence.** Focus on important factors that can be changed.
5. **Plan and iterate.** A work plan should evolve as evidence emerges.
6. **Use the right analytical tools.** Begin with simple methods and validate data before complex modeling.
7. **Synthesize, then recommend.** Findings must become a clear story and an actionable decision.
8. **Make uncertainty explicit.** Communicate assumptions, likelihood and limitations.
9. **Use collaboration and diverse perspectives.** They help challenge biases and improve solutions.

## Quick Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Hypothesis-led approach** | Define a problem, form hypotheses and organize work to test them |
| **Logic tree** | A visual or logical breakdown of a problem into component parts |
| **Disaggregation** | Breaking a complex problem into smaller, logical elements |
| **Prioritization** | Selecting the issues that matter most for the desired outcome |
| **Work plan** | Activities, owners, timelines and analyses needed to solve the problem |
| **Synthesis** | Combining findings into an overall message and implications |
| **Heuristic** | A practical rule or simple method used to guide analysis or decisions |
| **A/B testing** | Comparing alternatives through tests to learn which performs better |
| **Exploratory data analysis** | Examining and visualizing data to understand its patterns and characteristics |
| **Cognitive bias** | A systematic tendency that can influence judgment or decisions |
| **Design thinking** | A user-empathy-led, iterative approach involving idea generation, prototyping and feedback |
