# Defining the Problem – Quick Revision Notes

## 1. Why Problem Definition Comes First

Every problem-solving effort—whether it takes a few minutes or several months—starts by **defining the problem**.

A clear problem definition helps ensure that:
- You are solving the **right problem**, not just reacting to a symptom.
- Everyone involved agrees on what needs to be solved.
- The desired outcome and definition of success are clear.
- Time and resources are focused on the most relevant issues.

### Common mistakes when defining a problem

- **Relying on gut feeling:** Making quick judgments under pressure without checking assumptions.
- **Over-scoping:** Trying to solve a much larger problem than necessary or beyond available resources.
- **Ignoring stakeholders:** Forgetting the needs and priorities of the people affected by the solution.
- **Acting too quickly:** Moving into data collection or analysis before confirming what decision needs to be made.

**Key principle:** Slow down briefly to define the problem. This can help you reach the answer faster.

## 2. Align on What You Are Actually Solving

Different people may interpret the same problem differently.

For example, “What should we price this product at?” could mean:
- Set a price that encourages adoption.
- Set a price that maximizes profit.

Both are valid, but they lead to different analyses and recommendations.

Before starting, align on:
- The exact question to answer.
- What success looks like.
- Who the decision-maker is.
- The constraints and dependencies.
- The time available and precision required.

This is especially important for **in-the-moment decisions**, when people may assume everyone already understands the problem.

### Urgent decisions vs. frequent ad-hoc decisions

- **Urgent / crisis decisions:** High stress, high urgency and potentially serious consequences. The first challenge is often clarifying what outcome matters most.
- **Frequent, lower-consequence decisions:** Need a practical approach that can be applied quickly and repeatedly.

In either case, avoid letting urgency create a bias toward action before the problem is clear.

## 3. Learn from the Examples

### Example A – Missing warehouse payments

A warehouse manager notices that payments have stopped. After checking IT systems and the drivers' supervisor, he assumes the company has been hacked because he recently saw a TV report about hacking incidents.

**What went wrong?**
- He jumped to a conclusion based on a memorable example.
- He did not first define which part of the payment process had stopped working.
- He focused on one explanation instead of considering other possible causes.

**A better problem question:**

“How does the payment process work, and what part of that process is failing and causing payments to stop?”

Then identify possible contributing factors, rule out unlikely explanations, prioritize the most likely causes and test them systematically.

### Example B – DentMerc's Click*Brush market research

The team initially proposed spending ₹50 lakh to research many toothbrush purchase factors, including color, size, grip and packaging.

Arun asked questions and used insights from similar past projects:
- A previous floss project suggested that **customers' willingness to pay** was important.
- A previous brightening-strip project showed the importance of **performance specifications compared with competitors**.

The team narrowed the research focus to these critical questions and reduced the proposed research budget from ₹50 lakh to ₹15 lakh.

**Lesson:** Powerful questions, relevant past experience and a clear problem definition can reduce unnecessary scope, cost and effort.

## 4. The SMART Problem Question

After writing a concise problem statement, test it using **SMART**.

| SMART element | Question to ask | What it ensures |
|---|---|---|
| **Specific** | Is the question sufficiently narrow and detailed? | People understand exactly what needs to be solved |
| **Measurable** | How will we measure whether the problem is solved? | There is a clear measure or indicator of success |
| **Actionable** | Does the question point towards actions that can be taken? | The answer can lead to practical steps |
| **Relevant** | Is it relevant to the decision-maker and stakeholders? | Everyone is aligned on the goal |
| **Time-bound** | By when must the problem be solved? | The solution fits the required time horizon |

### Example: Improve a vague question

**Vague:** “Where do we want to go on vacation?”

**More SMART:** “Where do my wife and I want to go to have the best vacation within the next four months?”

The refined question clarifies:
- **Specific:** Who is going?
- **Measurable:** What outcome are we seeking?
- **Actionable:** Is the decision something we can act on?
- **Relevant:** Does it matter to the people involved?
- **Time-bound:** When should it happen?

### Why SMART matters

A SMART question defines the boundaries of the solution space and helps the team prioritize.

For example, instead of asking “How do we grow revenue next year?”, a more useful question may be:

“How can we increase revenue next year within the existing budget?”

The budget constraint rules out options such as simply hiring more salespeople or spending more on marketing, helping the team focus on realistic alternatives.

## 5. Applying SMART to a Business Decision

**Initial question:**

“Should DentMerc enter the well-established Southeast Asian toothbrush market with a new product, the Click*Brush?”

This question has several useful elements:
- **Specific:** Names the market and product.
- **Measurable:** Requires a go / no-go decision.
- **Actionable:** The answer leads to a decision to enter or not enter.
- **Relevant:** The decision is relevant to DentMerc.
- **Time-bound:** The timeline is not stated.

**Improved SMART question:**

“What market research can we gather and analyze within one month to best determine whether DentMerc should enter the well-established Southeast Asian toothbrush market with the Click*Brush?”

**Important:** A problem statement can be refined as the team learns more. It is an iterative process.

## 6. The Problem Statement Worksheet

The **Problem Statement Worksheet** helps a team examine the important elements of a problem, align stakeholders and define a practical solution space.

| Component | What to consider |
|---|---|
| **Problem question** | In one concise question, what must be resolved? Apply SMART. |
| **Context** | What is happening, and what background information matters? |
| **Scope** | What will and will not be considered? |
| **Constraints** | What can or cannot happen within the solution space? |
| **Success criteria** | What must happen for the solution to succeed? What could cause it to fail? |
| **Stakeholders** | Who makes decisions, supports or blocks the solution, or is affected by implementation? |
| **Resources / sources of insight** | Where can the team get information, and what existing work can be reused? |

### Scope vs. constraints

- **Scope:** The areas or possibilities included in the problem.
- **Constraints:** The limits or conditions that the solution must respect.

A good scope is broad enough to consider meaningful options but narrow enough to keep the work manageable.

### Success criteria

Define how success will be judged:
- What needs to happen?
- How will improvement be recognized?
- When should the solution be delivered?
- What does success look like to the key stakeholders?
- What could cause the solution to fail?

Criteria should be SMART wherever possible.

### Why use the worksheet?

- Aligns the team and wider stakeholders.
- Helps uncover overlooked options and sources of insight.
- Makes constraints and resources explicit.
- Improves later structuring and prioritization.
- Keeps the team focused on the right problem.

**Use it flexibly:** In a fast-moving situation, it may be a mental checklist rather than a form that must be fully completed. Refresh it as new information and stakeholders emerge.

## 7. Ask Powerful Questions

Good questions help challenge assumptions, clarify the decision and focus the work.

Useful questions include:
- What decision are we really trying to make?
- What factors contribute most to that decision?
- What must we know to make the decision?
- Which factors are essential, and which are less important?
- Have we faced a similar problem before?
- What did we learn from that earlier situation?
- Are those lessons applicable in the current context?
- What is in scope, and what is outside scope?
- What constraints are fixed, and which might be flexible?
- Who else needs to be involved?

**Practical technique:** Look for relevant past projects, but check whether their context is sufficiently similar before reusing their conclusions.

## 8. Debiasing: Recognize and Challenge Your Biases

**Debiasing** means using a structured approach to identify biases that may affect how a problem is defined and to reduce their influence.

Biases can act as useful mental shortcuts, but they can also cause us to:
- Confirm what we already believe.
- Ignore warning signs.
- Reuse an old solution even when the context has changed.
- Follow a leader's opinion without independent thinking.

### Three bias types and techniques

| Bias | Meaning | Debiasing technique |
|---|---|---|
| **Confirmation bias** | Looking for or interpreting evidence as support for an existing hypothesis | **Key assumption check:** List assumptions and test each one. **Analysis of competing hypotheses:** Consider multiple explanations and collect evidence for each. |
| **Confidence bias** | Being overconfident and disregarding warning signs | **Premortem:** Imagine the proposed solution has failed; work backwards to identify what went wrong and how it could have been prevented. |
| **Stability bias** | Holding on to a mindset or approach because it worked in the past | **Constraint release:** List constraints and relax them one by one to generate alternatives. **Point-of-view analysis:** Reframe the problem from the perspectives of four to six people with different views. |

### Additional debiasing practice: Challenger session

- Ask a person who strongly supports one perspective to argue the opposite view.
- Then reverse the roles.
- Use the exercise to expose blind spots and test assumptions.
- Encourage empathy for people who see the problem differently.

## 9. Key Takeaways

1. **Define before acting:** The first step is to clarify the problem, not immediately collect data or implement a solution.
2. **Align on success:** Different stakeholders may define the same problem and its desired outcome differently.
3. **Use SMART:** Make the problem specific, measurable, actionable, relevant and time-bound.
4. **Respect constraints:** Define the scope and limits before exploring solutions.
5. **Use the worksheet as a guide:** Consider context, scope, constraints, success criteria, stakeholders and resources.
6. **Ask better questions:** Use questions and relevant past experience to identify what really matters.
7. **Challenge gut reactions:** Test assumptions and consider alternative explanations.
8. **Keep refining:** Problem definition should evolve as understanding improves.

## Quick Revision: Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Problem statement / question** | A concise description of what needs to be resolved |
| **SMART** | Specific, Measurable, Actionable, Relevant and Time-bound |
| **Scope** | The areas or possibilities included in the problem |
| **Constraints** | Limits or conditions that the solution must work within |
| **Success criteria** | Conditions or measures used to judge whether the solution succeeds |
| **Stakeholder** | A person or group with an interest in, influence over or concern about the problem |
| **Debiasing** | Deliberately identifying and reducing the influence of biases |
| **Confirmation bias** | Favoring evidence that supports an existing belief or hypothesis |
| **Confidence bias** | Overconfidence that can cause warning signs to be ignored |
| **Stability bias** | Holding on to a familiar approach because it worked previously |
| **Premortem** | Imagining that a solution has failed and identifying possible reasons |
| **Constraint release** | Temporarily relaxing constraints to explore alternative solutions |
