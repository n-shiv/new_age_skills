# Problem Structuring, Prioritization & Better Decisions

## 1. Structure the Problem
- Break a complex problem into smaller, manageable parts.
- Use a clear structure to guide analysis and avoid exploring unrelated questions.
- For broad or unclear problems, test different ways of structuring them.
- Keep the structure iterative: refine it as new information emerges.

## 2. Prioritize Issues
Prioritize based on:
- **Impact:** How much could this issue affect the outcome?
- **Feasibility:** Can it be investigated or addressed with the available time and resources?
- **Likelihood:** When finding a root cause, how likely is each issue to be the cause?

Avoid:
- Investigating issues in a simple top-to-bottom order.
- Trying to analyze everything at once (analysis paralysis).
- Eliminating possible causes too early.

## 3. Issue Trees
An issue tree breaks the main problem into progressively smaller questions.

**How to build one**
1. Put the defined problem at the top.
2. Break it into major issues or questions.
3. Break each issue into its underlying drivers.
4. Continue until each endpoint can be addressed by a specific analysis.
5. Review and refine the tree.

Draft more than one structure when useful. The first version is rarely perfect.

## 4. MECE: No Overlaps, No Gaps
**MECE = Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive.**

- **Mutually Exclusive:** Each issue belongs to only one branch; avoid overlap.
- **Collectively Exhaustive:** Together, the branches cover the full problem; avoid gaps.

Examples:
- Revenue and cost are separate drivers of profit.
- Breakfast, lunch and meals are not MECE because meals overlaps with breakfast and lunch.
- Red and green traffic lights are not exhaustive because yellow is missing.

Build a broad enough tree first, then prioritize. Do not remove potential branches prematurely.

## 5. The 80/20 Principle
- A relatively small share of the work can often deliver most of the useful insight.
- Focus on analyses likely to move the problem toward an answer fastest.
- Reprioritize as findings emerge; disproving one branch may eliminate several follow-up analyses.
- The 80/20 rule is a guide, not a literal requirement to do only 20% of the work.
- Be comfortable moving forward when the evidence is sufficiently directional; the required precision depends on the decision.

## 6. Prioritization Matrix
Assess each issue or analysis on two dimensions:

| | Low feasibility | High feasibility |
|---|---|---|
| **High impact** | Explore ways to make it feasible; assess whether it is essential | Highest priority |
| **Low impact** | Lowest priority | Possible quick wins |

Use relative ratings such as high/medium/low when precise numbers are unavailable. For longer projects, agree on what impact and feasibility mean before ranking.

## 7. Solve Everyday Problems at the Source
- Small recurring problems (“sand”) can accumulate and disrupt daily work, even when they are not large projects (“rocks”).
- Involve the people closest to the work; they can describe the problem and its impact accurately.
- Compare the expected process with what actually happens.
- Test possible causes instead of jumping to conclusions.
- Validate the fix with the people affected.
- Keep asking **why** to reach the deeper root cause; an immediate fix may not solve the underlying system issue.
- Escalate when a barrier—such as budget or policy—requires leadership action. Leaders should enable the team, not simply prescribe the solution.
- Use a tactical implementation plan for longer-term changes, with steps, timing and owners.

## 8. Make Better Decisions: WRAP
A useful decision process should challenge assumptions and reduce predictable errors.

- **W — Widen your options:** Consider more than one alternative.
- **R — Reality-test assumptions:** Look for evidence that could disprove the preferred option; use comparable cases and experiments.
- **A — Attain distance:** Step back from the decision and reduce the influence of immediate emotions or attachment.
- **P — Prepare to be wrong:** Recognize uncertainty and consider a realistic range of outcomes, including the downside.

Other practical habits:
- Invite disagreement and watch for groupthink.
- Ask for a second-best option, not just the preferred recommendation.
- Use experiments to test ideas rather than relying only on persuasion or presentations.
- Create a process that helps decision makers get better advice; awareness of bias alone does not remove bias.

## Quick Revision
- **Structure:** Break the problem into smaller questions.
- **MECE:** No overlaps, no gaps.
- **Prioritize:** Focus on impact, feasibility and likelihood.
- **80/20:** Do the analyses that deliver the most useful insight.
- **Issue tree:** Map the problem from broad questions to analyzable endpoints.
- **Root cause:** Keep asking why; validate the solution.
- **WRAP:** Widen options, Reality-test assumptions, Attain distance, Prepare to be wrong.
